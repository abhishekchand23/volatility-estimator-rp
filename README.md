Volatility of security price returns is not directly observable, but it drives almost every decision in finance. As it is a latent variable, it must be inferred from available price data, either by modeling the latent state or by constructing nonparametric proxies from observed prices. When high-frequency data is available, summing intraday squared returns (“realized volatility”) reasonably estimates daily volatility, but they require careful treatment of market microstructure noise \citet{alizadeh2002RangeBasedEstimationStochastica}. Alternatively, within the class of daily volatility measures, extreme value (range-based) estimators exploit information in the day’s high and low, often together with the open and close (OHLC), to deliver substantial efficiency gains over close-to-close volatility while remaining data light.

Historically, the literature has advanced several influential OHLC estimators. For example, \citet{parkinson1980ExtremeValueMethod} introduced the “extreme value method”, showing that the high-low range under no drift geometric Brownian motion yields an estimator that is several times more efficient than the close-to-close benchmark. \citet{garman1980EstimationSecurityPrice} combined OHLC in a quadratic form that further reduces the estimator variance under the same benchmark. To address a key limitation of earlier zero-drift estimators \citet{rogers1991EstimatingVarianceHigh} introduced an OHLC estimator that is unbiased for any drift in intraday prices. They also showed that substituting discrete intraday high and low prices for the true continuous time extremes can induce substantial error, and they proposed a simple correction that nearly eliminates this discretization bias. Subsequently, \citet{yang2000DriftIndependentVolatilityEstimation} proposed a multi-period estimator that is unbiased, invariant to drift, and is consistent in the presence of opening price jumps. Henceforth, we denote the \citet{parkinson1980ExtremeValueMethod} estimator as \textbf{PK}, the \citet{garman1980EstimationSecurityPrice} estimator as \textbf{GK}, the \citet{rogers1991EstimatingVarianceHigh} estimator as \textbf{RS}, the \citet{yang2000DriftIndependentVolatilityEstimation} estimator as \textbf{YZ}, and the close-to-close estimator as \textbf{CC}.

Early empirical work done by \citet{beckers1983VariancesSecurityPrice} and \citet{wiggins1991EmpiricalTestsBias} revealed biases and inefficiencies in extreme value volatility estimators.  Later, \citet{kunitomo1992ImprovingParkinsonMethod} improved the \textbf{PK} estimator by incorporating non-zero drift into the geometric Brownian motion framework. Subsequently, \citet{alizadeh2002RangeBasedEstimationStochastica} demonstrated that ranges serve as  near-Gaussian and highly informative proxies for latent volatility, utilizing this insight  for efficient (quasi-) likelihood estimation in stochastic-volatility models. 

While seminal extreme volatility estimators are well understood individually, there is no unified procedure that enumerates all unbiased estimators available under a particular information set and price process, nor identifies the most efficient estimator among them. Although refinements exist, \citet{meilijson2009GarmanKlassVolatilityEstimator}, reweighting of OHLC quadratics that improve on the GK estimator, and \citet{saichev2009MostEfficientHomogeneous}, who derive the most efficient homogeneous estimators. There is no computational framework that characterizes the full family of unbiased range‑based estimators under stated assumptions and selects the minimum‑variance member. The paper provides a framework using null space decomposition of the unbiasedness constraints, coupled with a quadratic variance projection, which nests classical estimators and yields strictly more efficient alternatives when they are not optimal.
